Everyone expected high interest rates to cool home prices.
After inflation, prices have fallen since 2021, and the monthly payment is still up 25%.

Standard economic theory suggests that when mortgage rates jump, home prices should fall to offset borrowing costs. But the actual story is more complicated. Buyers do not pay a sticker price; they pay a monthly payment, and a lender caps that payment at a share of their income. In 2021–2023, higher rates drove the payment up while homes for sale stayed scarce: active listings averaged about half their 2017–2019 level and the homeowner vacancy rate fell to its lowest since 1956, even though builders started more single-family homes in 2021 than in any year since 2006. That left young buyers squeezed by both high prices and high financing costs. The figures below explain why.

−1% Median home price after inflation, 2021 → 2023 (+11% before inflation), even as mortgage rates more than doubled.
+37% Monthly mortgage payment after inflation, 2021 → 2023 (+54% before inflation).
+62% Real cost of renting (after inflation) since 1988, leaving less cash for a down payment.

The story, in six figures, follows four reasons buying a first home got harder (incomes, rates, qualifying, and saving) and what that did to homeownership among young adults.

Reason 1 · Incomes have not kept up with what lenders require

A lender caps the housing payment at 28% of gross income, which sets the income a buyer needs. In 2024 dollars, the median household aged 25–34 earned $86,663 in 2021 against $91,441 required; by 2024 it earned $90,100 against $119,105. After inflation its income rose 4% while the income required rose 30%.

Four Forces That Move House Prices

Before asking whether it is time to buy, start with the four forces that shape the price and the payment: location, housing markets and rates, macro trends, and debt and savings.

1. Location

Jobs, schools, transport, land, and local supply decide how much competition a buyer faces. A national average cannot tell you what a home costs in your city.

2. Housing market & rates

Inventory sets bargaining power, while mortgage rates change the monthly cost of every dollar borrowed.

3. Macro trends

Inflation, employment, and Federal Reserve policy shape wages, borrowing costs, and how much money households have left after essentials.

4. Debt & savings

Student loans, credit cards, rent, and the down payment all compete for the same paycheck and affect a lender's decision.

House Hacking: Offsetting Housing Cost With Rent

House hacking means buying a home with a second unit or spare rooms, living in one part, and renting out the rest. Rent from tenants helps cover the mortgage, so a buyer can own sooner and at a lower net monthly cost. It also makes the buyer a landlord, with the leverage, vacancy, and upkeep risks that come with that. Figures marked from our data come from this project's pipeline; the rest are cited under Sources.

Rent Has Outrun Young Households' Incomes

  • From our data: the Census median asking rent rose 64% after inflation from 1988 to 2024, while the real median income of householders aged 25–34 rose 20%. (The +62% at the top of the page runs to 2026; income data ends in 2024.)
  • From our data: over the same years, the median asking rent grew from 14% to 20% of that age group's median income, leaving less to save toward a down payment.
  • Rent pays for housing but builds no equity. An owner's payment does build some, through principal, but interest, taxes, insurance, and upkeep build none either.

Rent From a Second Unit Can Cover About Half the Cost of Owning

  • Illustration from our data: in 2024 the all-in monthly cost of owning the median-priced home, meaning principal, interest, taxes, insurance, and upkeep with 20% down, was $3,128. A second unit rented at that year's median asking rent of $1,487 would cover 48% of it, before vacancy, repairs, and time spent managing tenants.
  • Smaller down payments on a home you live in (Fannie Mae): conventional loans allow as little as 5% down on a 2- to 4-unit home when the buyer lives in one unit (since November 2023), and 3% down on a 1-unit home for first-time buyers or HomeReady borrowers. A property the buyer does not live in needs at least 15% down for 1 unit and 25% for 2 to 4 units.
  • A smaller down payment means a larger loan, a higher monthly payment than the 20%-down figure above, and mortgage insurance until enough equity builds up.

Owners Hold Far More Wealth, but Owning Is Not the Only Reason

  • Federal Reserve Survey of Consumer Finances (2022): the median net worth of homeowner families was $396,200, against $10,400 for renters and other families, about 38 times more. Homeowners also tend to be older and to earn more, so the gap is not caused by owning alone.
  • Principal paydown: on a $350,000 30-year fixed loan at 6.5%, about $3,900 of the first year's payments goes to reducing the balance; the other $22,600 or so is interest. When tenants' rent helps make those payments, it helps fund that equity.

Rental Deductions, and the Rules That Limit Them

IRS Publications 527 and 523:

  • If you rent out part of your home, you divide expenses such as mortgage interest, property taxes, and insurance between the rented part and the part you live in. The rented part's share is deducted from rental income.
  • The rented part of the building can be depreciated over 27.5 years. That lowers taxable rental income now, but gain equal to the depreciation is taxed when you sell; the exclusion for selling your home does not cover it.
  • Rental losses are passive losses. An owner who actively participates can deduct up to $25,000 a year of them against other income, and that allowance shrinks as modified adjusted gross income rises above $100,000, reaching zero at $150,000.

This is general information, not tax advice.

Takeaways & Buyer Action Plan

The best response is not to chase a headline price. The best response is to reduce debt, focus on total carrying cost, and enter markets where supply is expanding rather than where scarcity is still strongest.

Leverage Cuts Both Ways (5x–20x)

A down payment is a leveraged stake in the home's price. With 20% down, each 1% change in price is a 5% change in the buyer's stake; with 5% down, it is a 20% change.

  • Upside: a $400,000 home bought with 5% down ($20,000) that rises 4% in a year gains $16,000, or 80% of the down payment. From our data, 4% is about the median sales price's average yearly rise from 1984 to 2025, or about 1% after inflation.
  • Downside: a 5% fall erases the whole $20,000. From our data, the median sales price fell about 6% in both 2008 and 2009.
  • Neither figure counts closing costs, interest, mortgage insurance, upkeep, or the cost of selling.

Summary: Renting, Owning, and House Hacking

Component Renter Owner House Hacker
Monthly housing cost Rent; builds no equity Mortgage, taxes, insurance, upkeep; principal builds equity Same as an owner, partly offset by tenants' rent
Lowest conventional down payment None (a security deposit) 3% (1 unit, eligible buyers) 5% (2–4 units, living in one)
Tax treatment No housing deductions Mortgage interest and property taxes, if itemizing Also rental expenses and depreciation on the rented part; depreciation is taxed at sale
Main risks Rent increases; less left to save Price declines, repairs, selling costs An owner's risks, plus vacancy, unpaid rent, landlord duties, and more leverage

House hacking can lower the net cost of owning and let a buyer start with less cash, but it adds leverage and landlord risk. Whether it pays off depends on local rents, vacancy, the price paid, and the buyer's finances.

Prioritize Debt Reduction

Lowering credit card and student debt boosts your debt-to-income ratio and improves your qualification odds even before a lender looks at a mortgage rate.

Focus on PITI, Not Sticker Price

Mortgage affordability is driven by principal, interest, taxes, and insurance — the real monthly burden — not by an advertised price tag alone.

Look for Supply Expansion

Buyers can gain leverage where new construction, zoning flexibility, or greater inventory are expanding. That is where bargaining power and rate buydowns often become available.

Track Reentry Signals

The clearest future signals are slower mortgage-rate growth, rising active listings, and improving rent-to-income ratios. Those conditions bring the market back toward a more balanced buying environment.

Fewer Homes for Sale, More Homes Being Built

Each line compares a year with its own 2017–2019 average, set to 100. Listings count the homes for sale, mostly existing ones; starts count new single-family homes begun. During the 2021–2023 rate shock (shaded), listings fell to about half of normal while starts ran above it.

Macroeconomic Backdrop: inflation and rent growth ate away at the income a young household could save. These are the conditions that turn a mortgage into an affordability problem rather than a simple price problem.

Debt Burden & Qualifications: consumer debt was not what made qualifying harder. Student loan and credit card debt per person fell from 10.5% of young-household income in 2019 to 9.2% in 2024. What pushed households past the qualifying line was the housing payment itself, which rose from 30% to 40% of young-household income between 2021 and 2023.

Limitations, Bias & Ethical Considerations

These models are national and generalized. The actual experience differs by income, city, credit profile, family structure, and local market conditions.

Standardized Model Assumptions

The calculations assume a conventional 30-year fixed mortgage with 20% down and a 28% front-end debt-to-income threshold. Many first-time buyers use FHA, VA, or family support, so their actual experience can differ materially. The house hacking section discusses lower down payments, which these calculations do not model.

Aggregated Data Erases Local Disparities

National median prices smooth over widespread regional differences. Real affordability gaps remain large across race, income, and geography.

Before and After Inflation

Ratios such as the affordability index are unaffected by inflation, because both sides are in the same year's dollars. Dollar amounts and percentage changes are affected, so the page says which basis each uses. We adjust with the Consumer Price Index, which is about one-third housing costs; adjusting with CPI less shelter instead makes the rent finding stronger, +74% rather than +60% from 1988 to 2025. Years to save a down payment assumes the price holds still while you save, so if prices rise faster than savings grow, it takes longer.

House Hacking Draws on Outside Sources

The house hacking section adds Federal Reserve, Fannie Mae, and IRS sources to this project's data. Lending and tax rules change, and the figures reflect those sources as checked in September 2026. Being a landlord brings legal duties and risks that vary by state and city.

Decomposition Is Descriptive, Not Advice

The charts and decomposition explain market behavior from publicly available FRED and Census data. They are not a substitute for mortgage or financial advice.
